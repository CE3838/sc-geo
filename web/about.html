What the map shows

The geology is one seamless map merged from every public geologic map with usable GIS data: USGS and South Carolina Geological Survey (SCGS) quadrangles and compilations listed in the source catalog, plus the USGS State Geologic Map Compilation (SGMC). The Map units panel says how many maps were merged; the property card names the map behind each polygon.

Where maps overlap

Maps are ranked and each one is kept only where no better map already covers the ground:

  1. Most detailed first: the smallest scale denominator wins (1:24,000 beats 1:100,000).
  2. Then the newest map.
  3. Then the mapper's certainty (a unit identified as certain beats questionable).

The losing maps are not thrown away. For every merged polygon, the other maps under it are measured (their share of its area) and used for the agreement part of the confidence, and the ones that disagree are listed on the card as Other maps here with their citation, scale and how much of the polygon they cover. A map counts only where it covers more than 1% of a polygon.

SGMC (1:500,000) covers the whole state, so it fills in wherever no detailed map exists. Maps coarser than 1:1,000,000 are not used: they would never win anywhere and would only dilute agreement. SCGS FTP quadrangle packages with no scale in the catalog are taken as 1:24,000 (an inferred scale; every SCGS quadrangle package is published at that scale).

Two layers: Unconsolidated Deposits and Consolidated Rock

Units are split into two layers by the material at the surface, not by depth or province. A unit goes in Unconsolidated Deposits when its GeMS GeoMaterial names sediment, made land, water, regolith, alluvium, marsh, sand, clay and similar; in Consolidated Rock when it names rock, granite, gneiss, schist, volcanic or plutonic rock. Without a GeoMaterial the lithology decides (unconsolidated or water), and failing that the age: units younger than 66 Ma (Cenozoic) go in Unconsolidated Deposits. The split is an inference. Unconsolidated Deposits draw on top; turn them off to see the rock map under them where a map shows both.

The confidence value

Each merged polygon has a confidence from 0 to 1 that the unit shown is the right one. It is computed by merge/score.py from four things:

confidence = scale weight × identity weight × agreement factor + Geolex bonus (at most 1, rounded to 3 decimals)

Detail on the map sets the starting point. Scale weight by scale denominator:

Scale weight × identity weight is the base confidence.

3. Agreement of the other maps

Every other map under the polygon is compared with the winner by unit name, using USGS Geolex names (a facies or member is compared as its formation):

The other map's unitAgreement
Same Geolex unit1.00
Both units informal (no formal name), ages overlap0.50

Different named units, or ages do not overlap0.00

The agreement a is the area-weighted mean over the other maps (each map's share of the polygon is its weight, and a map counts once however many of its polygons lie under it). Then

agreement factor = 0.80 + 0.25 × a

so full disagreement multiplies the base by 0.80 and full agreement by 1.05. Where no other map covers the ground the factor is 0.92: a single map is neither confirmed nor contradicted. Water is left out on both sides, because maps differ there only in where the shoreline was drawn.

4. Published research (Geolex)

When the unit is a recognized USGS Geolex unit and its age does not contradict the Geolex age, 0.03 is added.

What the levels mean

High 0.70 or more: typically a detailed (1:24,000 to 1:100,000) map that other maps agree with.
Medium 0.40 to under 0.70: a regional map alone, or a detailed map that others dispute.
Low under 0.40: a map of unknown or very small scale, or strong disagreement.

With Color geology by: Confidence the map shades from red (0.3 and below) through yellow (0.6) to green (0.9 and above).

The confidence ranks how well supported the mapped unit is. It is not a probability, and it says nothing about the position of a contact on the ground, which is only as good as the map's scale (at 1:24,000 a 0.5 mm line is about 40 ft wide).

The card's "why" rows

Open Why 0.92 (the value of the polygon) next to the confidence badge on the property card to see the same four steps for the polygon you clicked: the scale weight, × the identity multiplier, × the agreement factor with how many other maps cover the ground and what share agree, + the Geolex bonus (or + 0), and the total. The card shows two decimals, so its rows may not multiply out exactly to the third decimal.

Worked example

A polygon of Wando Formation on a 1:24,000 map whose mapper marked it certain. An older 1:24,000 map also calls it Wando Formation over 60% of the polygon; SGMC covers all of it with an unnamed Pleistocene sediment unit.

Where only SGMC covers the ground and its unit has no Geolex name: 0.55 × 0.90 = 0.495, × 0.92 = 0.455, shown as 0.46 · Medium.

Inferred and derived values

A value read directly from a source is shown with its citation number, e.g. [1], which links to the reference list at the foot of the card. A value the software worked out is tagged inferred instead, with a note saying how (for example from the age text, ICS chart or derived class). Inferred values are stored with inferred=True and keep the source and page they were derived from.

Derived classes used for color and grouping

  • Age range (Ma): the printed age text turned into a range in millions of years on the ICS International Chronostratigraphic Chart. Ranges are written younger age first (late Pleistocene is 0.0117–0.129 Ma).
  • Age class: the epoch or period the age range overlaps most (an interval counts only when it holds more than 5% of the range). The legend has one Pleistocene class; the late, middle and early parts decide the overlap, and a range spanning all three is called Pleistocene. Each unit keeps its own refined age for the card.
  • Material class: GeoMaterial, lithology or unit name grouped into a short legend. Human-moved earth (fill, made land, dredge spoil) wins over any natural material another field names; valley and channel fill are natural deposits and are not counted as fill. Water is its own class (GeoMaterial or lithology "water"), and water polygons take no part in agreement.
  • Map units panel: units are grouped by period, then epoch, youngest first. Units with the same Geolex name are one entry listing every map label. Units named undivided or undifferentiated are one entry per epoch, Undifferentiated - <epoch>, which lists the printed names it includes.
  • Layer (Unconsolidated Deposits or Consolidated Rock), as described above.

Soil properties on the card are NRCS SSURGO representative estimates, not site measurements; the shrink-swell class is inferred from the linear extensibility (LEP) by the NRCS classes.

Values read from documents

Reports and maps with legally open full text are read by a model; code does everything else. Each value must come with the page it is on and a verbatim quote. The quote is then checked against the text of that page (tolerant of line breaks, hyphenation, ligatures and common OCR errors). A quote found only on a neighbouring page, or not at all, is not stored: it goes to the review queue (data/review/queue.json) for a person to check. Values whose second reading pass disagrees or is unclear, inexact quotes on pages with a real text layer, and water and rock test values with no printed units (units are never guessed), a value that is not a number, or a "|" where a digit belongs are queued as well.

confidence = S × M × Q × V × I
S, sourceagency, state survey or journal 0.85; other publishers 0.70; drafts at most 0.60. Map-unit descriptions on a map sheet, and values read from contour maps, take the map's scale weight (table above).
M, textPDF text layer 1.00; OCR text 0.90
Q, quote matchexact 1.00; OCR-tolerant 0.95; fuzzy 0.85

Text or image

Values read from the page text are stored with extraction_method llm. Values that exist only on a page image (map faces, cross sections, scanned figures) are stored as llm-image, with a locator to the exact box on the page, only after a second pass looks at the same image tile and agrees. Their confidence is min(0.60, S × 0.80) × V × I, times 0.70 when digits were unclear, so an image reading never outranks a checked text reading from the same source.

Elevations, coordinates and agreement

Printed locations are converted to latitude and longitude as a separate inferred value. Every elevation gets a derived NAVD88 value (NGVD29 through the NGS VERTCON grid); when the datum is not stated the value is kept as printed and marked approximate. Two surface elevations for the same unit and boundary from independent sources, at least one measured, within 1,000 ft and within 5 ft (plus 1 ft if either is approximate) agree. Each agreeing source raises confidence c to 1 − (1 − c)(1 − 0.25)k, at most 0.98. A disagreeing value is never dropped or lowered; both are kept as alternatives.

Subsurface surfaces and interpolation

Tops and bases of units, and water levels, are interpolated from point elevations in feet NAVD88 on SC State Plane coordinates (model/surfaces.py). This is what the coming cross-section and plane viewer will draw, so every estimate carries a ± uncertainty and a method, and is an inference citing every point it used.

Method

Three methods are tried on the same points and the one that predicts held-out points best is kept (leave-one-out cross-validation: each point is predicted from all the others and the errors compared, computed exactly, Dubrule 1983).

  • Ordinary kriging with at least 10 points. Pairs of points are binned (12 lag bins up to half the largest distance) into an empirical semivariogram, and spherical, exponential and gaussian models are fitted; the model with the lowest cross-validation error is kept.
  • Trend kriging (the regional dip), with the same minimum: a plane carries the regional seaward dip and the variogram, fitted to what is left, carries the local relief. The fitted dip, in feet per mile and its direction, is reported with the surface. A curved (quadratic) trend is not used: on the extracted tops it helped as often as it hurt.
  • Fallback: natural-neighbour (Sibson) interpolation inside the points' outline (convex hull) and inverse-distance weighting (power 2) outside it, used when there are too few points or no variogram can be fitted.

The kriging with the lower cross-validation error is kept unless it cross-validates more than 10% worse than the fallback (kriging is given that margin because its uncertainty grows with distance from the data). How well the variogram fits is not a reason to reject kriging: the variogram of a few tens of noisy points fits poorly even when kriging predicts well, and cross-validation is the judge.

The ± uncertainty

  • Kriging: the kriging standard deviation, calibrated so it matches the errors cross-validation actually saw (scaled by the square root of the mean standardized squared error, bounded to 0.25–20). About 95% of true values should fall within ±2 of it (checked on test surfaces).
  • Fallback: the method's leave-one-out RMSE, the same everywhere.
  • Never less than 0.3 ft, the error of elevations printed to the nearest foot.

Where there is no estimate

No value is given farther from the nearest point than the maximum distance (by default 3 times the median spacing between neighbouring points); the viewer will show a gap with the reason. Estimates outside the points' outline are flagged extrapolated.

Stacked units never cross

The tops of several units are put in order by geologic age (model/stack.py): each unit's age comes from its Geolex entry (the most specific interval it names, on the ICS chart; an inferred value), and a younger unit's top must lie at or above an older unit's top. Units whose age ranges overlap (two members of the same age) are ordered by superposition instead: one is kept above the other only when at least 3 logged holes show it above and none show the reverse; otherwise they are left unordered. Units with no known age are not moved, and an age order that as many holes show the other way up is not enforced. All of these are listed with the surfaces, and every moved value says whether its order came from age or from superposition (and how many holes).

Where two surfaces cross, both move to a single level between them, weighted so that the less certain surface moves more (the smallest change, weighted by uncertainty, that restores the order everywhere at once). The unit is then of zero thickness there. Every moved value is marked inferred with the amount it moved and why, and its ± becomes the largest ± of the surfaces it was reconciled with, never smaller than its own. The values before stacking are kept.

The comparison of methods on the extracted points (regional dip, anisotropy, contour maps as data, stacking), with the cross-validation results before and after these changes, is in docs/interpolation.md. Methods described there but not above (anisotropy, contour maps as data) are not used.
